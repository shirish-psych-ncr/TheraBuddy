Designing an OT & SLT Clinical Intelligence Web App
Product concept review · Clinical architecture · UX redesign · Progress tracking

Your proposed application has the foundations of something significantly more useful than a conventional therapy attendance tracker or digital session-note form.

The central opportunity is to create a longitudinal clinical documentation and decision-support system that helps occupational therapists (OTs) and speech-language therapists (SLTs) understand not merely what a child did, but:

What the child was able to do under specific conditions.

How much assistance was necessary.

Which environmental modifications improved participation.

Whether a skill is emerging, being maintained, or generalizing.

What changed relative to previous sessions.

What the next therapist should know before seeing the child.

For an early intervention centre such as Aasha, this could connect therapist workflows, individualized intervention plans, session records, caregiver communication, and management reporting in one application.

However, I would not implement your proposed screens exactly as written. The clinical content is extensive, but the information architecture needs considerable refinement.

The biggest issue is that your design currently combines five different functions into one mandatory workflow:

Baseline assessment and developmental profiling.

Session-entry observations.

Treatment planning and environmental accommodations.

Activity-level performance measurement.

Clinical interpretation and progress reporting.

These should share the same database, but they should not all be repeated every session.

Important clinical refinements
Autonomic terminology. I would retain the useful concept of observing regulatory states but remove the implication that a therapist can identify particular vagal pathways through behavioural observation alone. The neurophysiological claims of Polyvagal Theory are disputed, including in recent peer-reviewed analyses. 

PubMed
+1

Terminology and scope. Terms such as selective mutism, akathisia, apraxia, hypotonia, retained primitive reflexes, hallucinations, and OCD should not function as casual diagnostic checkboxes. Some can be established findings, others possible signs requiring assessment. Similarly, PDA is not a standalone DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 diagnosis.

Sensory behaviours. Stimming, solitary play, eye contact differences, and other neurodevelopmental variations are not inherently pathological or treatment targets. The app should prioritize distress, access, safety, meaningful participation, and the child's own needs.

Physical interventions. Physical prompting, therapist-assisted movement, deep pressure, weighted equipment, and crisis containment require separate consent, safety, and supervision considerations. They should never be treated as interchangeable response levels.

For safety concerns, the product should display clear escalation guidance defined by the centre and its supervising clinicians, rather than automatically diagnosing or prescribing actions.

The analytical engine must also distinguish:

Accuracy ≠ Independence ≠ Generalization ≠ Functional benefit.

A child might perform an activity correctly in 90% of trials while needing continuous modeling. Another might achieve 70% without prompts, across different people and environments.

Both observations matter. Neither should be compressed into a single percentage.

The professional literature likewise emphasizes measurable, goal-linked data and consistent measurement methods over time.

7. Features that would make the app genuinely valuable
Session-to-session clinical memory

Before starting a session, the therapist sees what worked last time, which prompts were needed, which goals remain active, and what needs to be revisited. Information should be separated into verified facts, earlier observations, and hypotheses.

Cross-disciplinary coordination

The OT can document the child's response to movement activities, while the SLT records communication performance during comparable activities. The system can show correlations worth investigating without assuming one intervention caused the other outcome.

Assisted clinical documentation

Generate an editable SOAP-style session note from recorded facts. The system should distinguish observations from interpretations, never fabricate missing measurements, and require therapist review before signing.

Pattern discovery

Surface associations such as improved task engagement when visual schedules were used, changes in cue dependence, or differences across environments. Display sample sizes, context, and uncertainty rather than claiming a treatment effect.

Automatic longitudinal reports

Produce monthly and quarterly summaries containing measurable progress, skills emerging, accommodations used, plateau or regression indicators, caregiver priorities, and recommendations for clinical review.

Clinical governance

Support sign-offs, supervising-clinician review, report versioning, change history, scope-of-practice permissions, and alerts for incomplete or contradictory documentation.

11. The most important design decision
There are two fundamentally different products you could build from this concept.

Product A: Digital Clinical Documentation

A therapist records observations, selects activities, tracks progress, and generates reports. This is achievable with conventional software and a carefully designed clinical content library.

Product B: Clinical Decision-Support and Intelligence Platform

The application compares interventions across sessions, detects patterns, evaluates goal trajectories, suggests hypotheses for therapist consideration, and supports individualized clinical planning.

Product B is substantially more ambitious. It would require more rigorous measurement design, clinician validation, data-quality controls, bias checks, and clear limitations around clinical recommendations.

I would architect for Product B, but initially build and validate Product A. That provides a useful application immediately while accumulating the structured, high-quality data required for more sophisticated analysis.

One further refinement is essential: the baseline evaluation should not be called Screen 1 of every session. It should be a persistent, versioned clinical profile. Every daily session should then become a new observation linked to that profile, not an isolated form.

That single architectural choice determines whether you end up with a collection of digital checklists or a meaningful clinical information system.

The next design artifact worth developing is the complete screen-by-screen functional specification: exact fields, conditional visibility, OT/SLT-specific activity templates, scoring rules, database relationships, and sample generated session reports.

10. Session workflow — from arrival to clinical report
I would redesign the daily workflow around the practical demands of a 45-minute session.

Select child and confirm attendance

Appointment, child identity, therapist, room and schedule automatically populated. Log arrival, absence, delay or cancellation.

Clinical pre-session snapshot

Review precautions, recent OT/SLT notes, current goals, prior successful accommodations and outstanding concerns. Record today's relevant presentation.

Identify observations

Check observed behaviours, abilities, difficulties and contextual factors. Mark meaningful changes from the child's baseline.

Review suggested interventions

The engine retrieves applicable strategies, goals, activities, equipment and relevant previous outcomes. Therapist chooses or creates the plan.

Conduct and document activities

For each activity, record opportunities, successful responses, independence, prompts, engagement, adaptations and any clinically significant events.

Capture exit state and handoff

Record end-of-session participation, changes, key achievements, barriers, and information relevant to the next therapist.

Generate and verify clinical note

Draft a factual summary, distinguish observations from interpretations, allow edits, require clinician sign-off, and store the finalized version.

A therapist must be able to interrupt the workflow, save a partial session, switch activities, record an unscheduled event, or finish notes afterward. Missing fields should not silently become negative findings.

15. The intelligence system should have three layers
Layer 1 — Deterministic rules

Validates age/access considerations, equipment availability, eligibility, contraindications, session overlaps, measurement definitions and required permissions. Explanations are traceable.
Layer 2 — Clinical knowledge retrieval

Uses professionally curated links between observations, functional goals, interventions, activities, equipment and evidence. Shows why an option appeared.
Layer 3 — Assisted clinical intelligence

Drafts summaries, finds comparable prior sessions, identifies possible patterns and highlights questions for clinician review. Never independently diagnoses or changes treatment.
Initially, I'd use transparent rule-based retrieval and a curated content library before introducing machine-learned rankings. That makes the early system easier to audit, test and correct.

Every proposed intervention should expose:

Why it was retrieved.

Which observations and active goals are linked.

Supporting clinical resources and their review dates.

What information about the child is missing.

Relevant precautions and prerequisites.

Who must approve its use.

How to measure whether it helped.

The therapist should also have a Not appropriate / Not now / Already tried / Worked previously feedback option, with a short reason. That feedback can improve the child's future shortlist without converting anecdotal outcomes into general clinical evidence.

A therapist opens Child A, checks difficulty transitioning between activities, and sees that the child has an active transition goal. The platform retrieves relevant options from its reviewed intervention library, presents a visual-transition activity that uses materials already available at the centre, and shows how the child responded when a similar activity was previously used.

The therapist chooses the intervention, adapts its difficulty, conducts it, records the outcome, and signs the session note. A subsequent SLT session can access the relevant functional handoff. The clinical master sees the longitudinal goal trajectory while the operations dashboard independently records both completed sessions and both therapists' attendance.

That is the cohesive product to aim for: not just a therapy recorder, not just an equipment catalogue, and not merely an AI suggestion engine—but an integrated clinical and operational system in which every observation, intervention, activity, resource and outcome has a meaningful relationship.

The most valuable next technical deliverable is a developer-ready functional specification and relational schema, followed by the first curated OT/SLT observation–intervention–activity–equipment mappings. Those mappings are the clinical foundation on which the suggestion engine will depend.

This online website/app should be usable by any centre with onboarding deciding future states. Therapists and children must be onboarded, as with equipments selection.

5. Child onboarding
Child onboarding should be incremental, not an intimidating complete assessment before the child can appear on the schedule.

I would define four stages:

Registration: Basic identity, DOB, caregiver contacts, preferred language, child ID and consent requirements.

Clinical intake: Referral reason, presenting concerns, relevant developmental and medical history, safety needs and existing assessments.

Care-team assignment: OT/SLT services, primary therapists, supervising clinician, session frequency and schedule.

Clinical baseline: Initial assessment, functional goals, measurement methods, intervention plan and review schedule.

Each child can have a clear onboarding state:

Registered → Intake pending → Assessment underway → Active care plan → Active therapy → Review / Discharge

The system should also support waiting-list, paused, transferred and archived states independently of clinical progress.

A child should be registrable before the assessment is complete, but the app should not treat missing baseline information as evidence of normal functioning. Clinical suggestions must account for what has and has not been assessed.

For repeat visits, the child profile should populate therapist session forms automatically, rather than requiring repeated demographic or developmental history entry.

13. Product principles to lock in
The application should be universal at the platform level, configurable at the organization level, specialized at the discipline level, and individualized at the child level.

That hierarchy is the most important architectural principle.

It means the software needs five separate configuration layers:

Platform defaults: Universal content, data structures, security requirements.

Organization configuration: Enabled services, workflows, centres, policies and defaults.

Branch configuration: Physical rooms, local inventory, working hours and staff deployment.

Therapist configuration: Discipline, credentials, permissions, assignments and preferred workflow settings.

Child configuration: Needs, history, goals, safety considerations, care team and individualized therapy plan.

Each layer inherits appropriate defaults from the preceding layer but can be customized within permitted boundaries.

The goal is that a newly registered SLT-only centre sees a clean speech-language therapy application, while a multidisciplinary centre sees a larger integrated system—both powered by exactly the same underlying product.

For development, I would next formalize the onboarding state machine, permission model, configuration inheritance rules, and normalized database schema. Those foundations must precede the intervention suggestion engine; otherwise each newly added discipline, role, or centre configuration risks requiring extensive changes throughout the application.

For a clinical app, do not implement offline mode by simply saving unencrypted patient information in browser local storage. Shared devices, stolen phones, expired sessions and unresolved edits create real confidentiality risks.

I'd release basic offline support first. Identifiable clinical offline storage should only be enabled after encryption, device/session controls, cache expiration and conflict resolution have been tested.

7. What about free AI intervention suggestions?
You can build the most important parts of your clinical suggestion engine without paying for an AI API.

The starting approach:

Observed need → Functional goal → Reviewed intervention → Activity variants → Equipment availability → Therapist selection

This works using relational database queries and curated clinical mappings.

For example, a therapist selects difficulty following a predictable activity transition. The system retrieves the child's active transition goal and any clinically reviewed strategies linked to it. It then checks which activities and materials the centre has enabled.

No language model is required.

Later you can add local/open-source models for drafting reports or searching clinical content. However, locally run models still require suitable hardware, and hosted inference has computational costs. Any clinical narrative generated by AI should be verified and signed by the responsible clinician.

8. How to ensure there are no surprise bills
I would impose these development rules from the beginning.

Do not enable pay-as-you-go infrastructure automatically.

Do not link billing accounts unless explicitly necessary and approved.

Do not implement chargeable SMS, WhatsApp or external AI features in the core application.

Make quota usage observable to the platform administrator.

Alert administrators before reaching hosting or database thresholds.

Set hard application-level request, attachment-size and storage limits.

Provide database export and migration tools from the beginning.

Test backups and restoration, not merely backup creation.

Keep cloud-provider integration isolated so it can be replaced.

Reject new uploads safely rather than silently discarding information when limits are reached.

A hard spending cap protects against charges, but does not protect against service interruption. Free quotas can stop accepting requests, which makes recovery and business-continuity planning essential.

11. The most important design decision
There are two fundamentally different products you could build from this concept.

Product A: Digital Clinical Documentation

A therapist records observations, selects activities, tracks progress, and generates reports. This is achievable with conventional software and a carefully designed clinical content library.

Product B: Clinical Decision-Support and Intelligence Platform

The application compares interventions across sessions, detects patterns, evaluates goal trajectories, suggests hypotheses for therapist consideration, and supports individualized clinical planning.

Product B is substantially more ambitious. It would require more rigorous measurement design, clinician validation, data-quality controls, bias checks, and clear limitations around clinical recommendations.

I would architect for Product B, but initially build and validate Product A. That provides a useful application immediately while accumulating the structured, high-quality data required for more sophisticated analysis.

4. Observation records should capture context, not merely presence
For every checked item, the therapist should optionally record:

Duration/recovery: When clinically relevant.

10. Session workflow — from arrival to clinical report
I would redesign the daily workflow around the practical demands of a 45-minute session.

Select child and confirm attendance

Appointment, child identity, therapist, room and schedule automatically populated. Log arrival, absence, delay or cancellation.

Clinical pre-session snapshot

Review precautions, recent OT/SLT notes, current goals, prior successful accommodations and outstanding concerns. Record today's relevant presentation.

Identify observations

Check observed behaviours, abilities, difficulties and contextual factors. Mark meaningful changes from the child's baseline.

Review suggested interventions

The engine retrieves applicable strategies, goals, activities, equipment and relevant previous outcomes. Therapist chooses or creates the plan.

Conduct and document activities

For each activity, record opportunities, successful responses, independence, prompts, engagement, adaptations and any clinically significant events.

Capture exit state and handoff

Record end-of-session participation, changes, key achievements, barriers, and information relevant to the next therapist.

Generate and verify clinical note

Draft a factual summary, distinguish observations from interpretations, allow edits, require clinician sign-off, and store the finalized version.

A therapist must be able to interrupt the workflow, save a partial session, switch activities, record an unscheduled event, or finish notes afterward. Missing fields should not silently become negative findings.

Dedicated OT and SLT views
The session engine can be shared, but the input controls should adapt by discipline.

OT: Trial counting, duration, assistance level, bilateral coordination, task steps, functional ADL sequence, motor accuracy, participation, activity adaptation.

SLT: Correct responses/opportunities, spontaneous versus prompted initiations, phoneme/word/utterance measures, communicative functions, language samples, AAC access and use, conversational turns, cueing hierarchy.

Integrated therapy: One activity may contribute separate measurements to two goals. Each clinician's contribution, role and sign-off remain clearly attributed.

15. The intelligence system should have three layers
Layer 1 — Deterministic rules

Validates age/access considerations, equipment availability, eligibility, contraindications, session overlaps, measurement definitions and required permissions. Explanations are traceable.
Layer 2 — Clinical knowledge retrieval

Uses professionally curated links between observations, functional goals, interventions, activities, equipment and evidence. Shows why an option appeared.
Layer 3 — Assisted clinical intelligence

Drafts summaries, finds comparable prior sessions, identifies possible patterns and highlights questions for clinician review. Never independently diagnoses or changes treatment.
Initially, I'd use transparent rule-based retrieval and a curated content library before introducing machine-learned rankings. That makes the early system easier to audit, test and correct.

Every proposed intervention should expose:

Why it was retrieved.

Which observations and active goals are linked.

Supporting clinical resources and their review dates.

What information about the child is missing.

Relevant precautions and prerequisites.

Who must approve its use.

How to measure whether it helped.

The therapist should also have a Not appropriate / Not now / Already tried / Worked previously feedback option, with a short reason. That feedback can improve the child's future shortlist without converting anecdotal outcomes into general clinical evidence.

One child, one longitudinal clinical record, with discipline-specific authoring and controlled sharing.

One activity, many potential domains and goals, with measurable variants.

One observation, multiple possible interpretations, never an automatic diagnosis.

Every measurement has a defined method, preserving comparability across sessions.

Every clinical decision remains attributable, reviewable and auditable.

Clinical outcomes and operations remain analytically distinct, despite sharing the same data infrastructure.

The system works without AI, with AI serving as an optional, supervised enhancement.

The defining interaction
A therapist opens Child A, checks difficulty transitioning between activities, and sees that the child has an active transition goal. The platform retrieves relevant options from its reviewed intervention library, presents a visual-transition activity that uses materials already available at the centre, and shows how the child responded when a similar activity was previously used.

The therapist chooses the intervention, adapts its difficulty, conducts it, records the outcome, and signs the session note. A subsequent SLT session can access the relevant functional handoff. The clinical master sees the longitudinal goal trajectory while the operations dashboard independently records both completed sessions and both therapists' attendance.

That is the cohesive product to aim for: not just a therapy recorder, not just an equipment catalogue, and not merely an AI suggestion engine—but an integrated clinical and operational system in which every observation, intervention, activity, resource and outcome has a meaningful relationship.

The most valuable next technical deliverable is a developer-ready functional specification and relational schema, followed by the first curated OT/SLT observation–intervention–activity–equipment mappings. Those mappings are the clinical foundation on which the suggestion engine will depend.

This online website/app should be usable by any centre with onboarding deciding future states. Therapists and children must be onboarded, as with equipments selection.

5. Child onboarding
Child onboarding should be incremental, not an intimidating complete assessment before the child can appear on the schedule.

I would define four stages:

Registration: Basic identity, DOB, caregiver contacts, preferred language, child ID and consent requirements.

Clinical intake: Referral reason, presenting concerns, relevant developmental and medical history, safety needs and existing assessments.

Care-team assignment: OT/SLT services, primary therapists, supervising clinician, session frequency and schedule.

Clinical baseline: Initial assessment, functional goals, measurement methods, intervention plan and review schedule.

Each child can have a clear onboarding state:

Registered → Intake pending → Assessment underway → Active care plan → Active therapy → Review / Discharge

The system should also support waiting-list, paused, transferred and archived states independently of clinical progress.

A child should be registrable before the assessment is complete, but the app should not treat missing baseline information as evidence of normal functioning. Clinical suggestions must account for what has and has not been assessed.

For repeat visits, the child profile should populate therapist session forms automatically, rather than requiring repeated demographic or developmental history entry.

Operational readiness: Can it safely and correctly perform the action being requested?

13. Product principles to lock in
The application should be universal at the platform level, configurable at the organization level, specialized at the discipline level, and individualized at the child level.

That hierarchy is the most important architectural principle.

It means the software needs five separate configuration layers:

Platform defaults: Universal content, data structures, security requirements.

Organization configuration: Enabled services, workflows, centres, policies and defaults.

Branch configuration: Physical rooms, local inventory, working hours and staff deployment.

Therapist configuration: Discipline, credentials, permissions, assignments and preferred workflow settings.

Child configuration: Needs, history, goals, safety considerations, care team and individualized therapy plan.

Each layer inherits appropriate defaults from the preceding layer but can be customized within permitted boundaries.

The goal is that a newly registered SLT-only centre sees a clean speech-language therapy application, while a multidisciplinary centre sees a larger integrated system—both powered by exactly the same underlying product.

For development, I would next formalize the onboarding state machine, permission model, configuration inheritance rules, and normalized database schema. Those foundations must precede the intervention suggestion engine; otherwise each newly added discipline, role, or centre configuration risks requiring extensive changes throughout the application.

6. Offline use and cross-device synchronization
Offline capability matters because therapists may lose connectivity while recording a session.

I would support two different offline levels.

Basic offline mode: The application shell, previously downloaded non-sensitive clinical library, and selected templates remain accessible.

Secure offline clinical drafts: A therapist can continue entering session observations during a connection interruption, with data stored locally under a carefully designed security policy and synchronized after reconnection.

Therapist records session

Local encrypted draft queue

Pending changes identified by unique operation IDs

Authenticated synchronization

Revalidate permissions · Detect conflicts · Prevent duplicate records

Authoritative server record

Available to the appropriate centre users on other devices

For a clinical app, do not implement offline mode by simply saving unencrypted patient information in browser local storage. Shared devices, stolen phones, expired sessions and unresolved edits create real confidentiality risks.

I'd release basic offline support first. Identifiable clinical offline storage should only be enabled after encryption, device/session controls, cache expiration and conflict resolution have been tested.

7. What about free AI intervention suggestions?
You can build the most important parts of your clinical suggestion engine without paying for an AI API.

The starting approach:

Observed need → Functional goal → Reviewed intervention → Activity variants → Equipment availability → Therapist selection

This works using relational database queries and curated clinical mappings.

For example, a therapist selects difficulty following a predictable activity transition. The system retrieves the child's active transition goal and any clinically reviewed strategies linked to it. It then checks which activities and materials the centre has enabled.

No language model is required.

Later you can add local/open-source models for drafting reports or searching clinical content. However, locally run models still require suitable hardware, and hosted inference has computational costs. Any clinical narrative generated by AI should be verified and signed by the responsible clinician.

8. How to ensure there are no surprise bills
I would impose these development rules from the beginning.

Do not enable pay-as-you-go infrastructure automatically.

Do not link billing accounts unless explicitly necessary and approved.

Do not implement chargeable SMS, WhatsApp or external AI features in the core application.

Make quota usage observable to the platform administrator.

Alert administrators before reaching hosting or database thresholds.

Set hard application-level request, attachment-size and storage limits.

Provide database export and migration tools from the beginning.

Test backups and restoration, not merely backup creation.

Keep cloud-provider integration isolated so it can be replaced.

Reject new uploads safely rather than silently discarding information when limits are reached.

A hard spending cap protects against charges, but does not protect against service interruption. Free quotas can stop accepting requests, which makes recovery and business-continuity planning essential.
